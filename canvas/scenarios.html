OryonIQ outreach · how a lead actually travels

Three doors, one corridor

A lead can arrive three ways — typed by a person, uploaded as a spreadsheet, or found by Apollo. After the door, all three walk the same corridor and meet the same gates. Learn the corridor once and every scenario is the same story with a different first step.

The model

The corridor is the part that matters

Nothing about verification, drafting or sending changes with the door. That is deliberate: an Apollo lead is not more trusted than a typed one, so it passes the same suppression check and the same verification. A lead is only ever refused by a gate, never by which door it used.

Solid blue is live today. Dashed amber is built-but-unwired. Dashed grey is built and disconnected. The corridor is entirely solid — every gap is at a door or in the return loop.
Scenario by scenario

What happens, and what stops it

Each door differs only in how a lead reaches the queue. The "where it stops" list under each is the honest one — these are refusals by design, not faults.

A · A person types a lead works today the only door open right now

Staff put a row into the Inbox tab in whatever shape their source gave them. Column names do not need to match anything — about sixty spellings are recognised, and if a required field is missing and there are unfamiliar columns, OpenAI is asked to find it. A row with no email address is left completely alone, because the poller fires while a person is still typing.

  1. Within 2 minutes the mapper claims the row and normalises it.
  2. Seconds later the gate runs: already in Leads? on the suppression list? then Reoon verifies the mailbox.
  3. Within 3 minutes the runner picks up one ready lead, drafts it, builds the page, and sends.
  4. About 5 minutes door to inbox. Measured, not estimated — this is the 1 September test run.
Where it legitimately stops
  • Product blank — refused outright rather than guessed. A wrong guess sends the wrong company's pitch to a real person.
  • Catch-all domain (your own included) — Reoon cannot confirm the mailbox exists, so it parks at needs_review until a person vouches.
  • Address invalid — dropped permanently, never sent.
  • Already in Instantly anywhere — silently no-ops. This is workspace-wide across all 14 campaigns.
B · A spreadsheet is uploaded to build · small the boss's file, or any list

This needs less than it sounds. The queue already accepts arbitrary column shapes — that is exactly what the alias table exists for — so an upload is not a new pipeline, it is a new way of filling the same queue. Every row then behaves precisely as scenario A.

  1. Drop the file — CSV or Excel, whatever columns it has.
  2. Rows land in the queue as one batch, each tagged with who uploaded it and when.
  3. The same gate runs per row — dedupe, suppression, Reoon. Nothing is trusted because it arrived in bulk.
  4. Bad rows come back with a reason against the original line, so the uploader fixes that row rather than the whole file.
What to expect
  • A 500-row file does not mean 500 emails. It means 500 verifications, then the daily cap decides the rest — see the limits below.
  • Verification costs a Reoon credit per row, and duplicates are removed before Reoon, so re-uploading the same file is cheap.
  • A file with no email column is refused as a file, not silently half-imported.
C · Apollo finds them built, disconnected the answer to "nobody is typing"

Search by title, industry and geography; keep only people who already have an email; reveal contact details for the survivors only. The credit discipline is already written — free search first, paid reveal last. What is missing is one connection: the sourcing workflow does not currently hand its results to the gate, so Apollo leads would bypass dedupe, suppression and Reoon. That is why it is switched off rather than half-wired.

  1. A search runs on a schedule — your ICP: capture and BD leaders at US government contractors.
  2. Free filtering first — anyone without an email is discarded before a credit is spent.
  3. Reveal the survivors then hand them to the same queue as a typed row.
  4. Identical corridor from there. An Apollo lead gets no more trust than one somebody typed.
Before this can be switched on
  • Route sourcing through the gate — roughly a day, and the single highest-value piece of wiring left.
  • Decide the ICP precisely. A vague search spends reveal credits on people you would never email.
  • Raise the daily cap, or Apollo will find far more than the pipeline will send.

The one number that blocks your target

You want 50–100 sends a day. The code caps it at 20. Instantly could do 120 tomorrow, so nothing needs buying — but the cap is a deliberate safety rail and raising it is a decision, not a fix. Raise it in steps and watch the bounce rate: a cold domain that suddenly sends five times its usual volume is the classic way to get a sending domain burned. My recommendation is 20 → 40 → 80, a week at each, once Apollo is supplying real leads.

The recommendation is custom, and the reason is narrow. Every other option leaves at least two tabs open, because none of them can show what Instantly, Reoon, Sendr and HubSpot each know about one person. That single-screen requirement is the whole point, and it is only possible once all four write into one database — which they now can. If you want something before that exists, HubSpot is the honest interim: your boss already lives there, and it costs nothing to try.
